# 13. Community Strategy — deliberation on the seven ideas

> Rob and his brother's brainstorm, assessed 23 Aug 2026. Research-grounded.
> Verdicts are recommendations; the calls are Rob's.

---

## 13.0 The strategic fork you have to resolve first

Everything else depends on this, so it goes first.

**Two weeks ago the brand analysis concluded that the product's load-bearing idea is that
nobody checks.** The disclaimer says it, the button says MARK CLEARED, and the argument was
that a self-attested order is the whole point — the record stands on your word.

**The CTR idea says the community checks.** Those are not compatible as stated. You cannot
simultaneously sell "your word is the only thing holding this up" and "your attempt is
reviewed by strangers before it counts."

You must pick one. But there is a third option that keeps almost everything you want:

> ### Witness, not verification.
> A witness **attests that they saw it**. A reviewer **judges whether it was good enough**.
> Honour traditions are full of witnesses and have almost no reviewers.

An Iron Ring obligation is witnessed. A judo grading has a panel. A duel had seconds. In none
of those does the witness *score* you — their presence is what makes the claim public, and
therefore costly to falsify.

**This resolves the contradiction.** Reframe CTR from *approval* to *attestation*, and the
honour frame survives intact while you get the community spine you want. It also removes most
of the legal and operational problems, as below.

| Verification framing | Witness framing |
|---|---|
| "Did they do it well enough?" | "Did someone see this?" |
| Creates a judging class | Creates a duty |
| Rejection is a failure | There is no rejection, only attestation or none |
| Disputes about standards | Disputes are rare — you either witnessed or you didn't |
| Encourages performing for the camera | Encourages doing the thing |

I would take witness. Everything below assumes it, and I flag where the choice changes things.

## 13.1 The seven ideas, assessed

### ① Community difficulty ranking (1–10) pre-launch → **ADOPT. Strongest idea of the seven.**

**Why it works, with precedent.** This is **consensus grading**, and it is how rock climbing
has graded routes for a century: a first ascensionist proposes a grade, the community adjusts
it through repeated ascents, and consensus becomes the number. It is trusted *because* it is
collective, it is self-correcting, and it creates enormous attachment — climbers argue about
grades for decades, and that arguing is community glue rather than friction. Map-ranking
queues in osu! and Beat Saber work the same way.

**Why it fits you specifically:**
- It is the only idea here that is **strictly better done before launch than after**. You are ordering 57 trials and you currently have no data at all on relative difficulty. You are guessing.
- It gives a pre-launch cohort a **real job**. People who shaped the product do not churn like people who bought it.
- Zero legal exposure, no UGC, no moderation, no storage cost.
- It is brand-perfect. A register that grades its own trials by consensus is *more* austere, not less.

**How to run it:**
- Recruit a **pilot cohort of 20–40**, free access, explicit deal: you get the trials early, you grade them, your grade shapes the final order.
- Grade on **two axes, not one**: *physical demand* (1–10) and *life friction* (1–10 — time, equipment, whether it needs other people). These come apart badly. "Walk for three hours" is low demand and high friction; "five rounds of seven minutes" is the reverse. A single 1–10 conflates them and gives you a bad ordering.
- Collect **completion rate and time-to-clear** alongside the rating — behaviour beats opinion when they disagree.
- Publish the final grades permanently on each trial. **No competitor publishes difficulty.**

**Reserve bibs 001–057 for this cohort.** The brand work already recommended conferring those
rather than issuing them. Grading the trials is exactly the service that earns one.

### ② Charity portal, 90 / 5.7 / 4.3 → **DO NOT BUILD AS DESIGNED.**

I have to be blunt, because the exposure here is criminal rather than civil.

**What the law says.** Under **Part II of the Charities Act 1992**, a business carrying on a
non-fundraising business that represents contributions will benefit a charity is a
**commercial participator**. That triggers:

- A **written agreement with each charity**, in prescribed form, before soliciting anything.
- A **statutory statement** on every solicitation disclosing the proportion the charity receives.
- **Breach is a criminal offence** punishable by fine.

Your 4.3% is squarely inside this. It is doable — plenty of businesses are commercial
participators — but it means a signed agreement with **every single charity** a member wants
to support, which destroys the "pick any charity" portal operationally.

**The 5.7% to the member is the bigger problem, and not primarily legally.** Paying an
individual a percentage of donations they personally solicited sits close to commission-based
fundraising by a **professional fundraiser**, regulated under the same Part II with its own
disclosure duties. And setting law aside:

> A donor gives £100 believing they are helping a charity. £90 reaches it. £5.70 goes to the
> person who asked them. £4.30 goes to a fitness app.

That is a headline, and it is the kind that ends brands. JustGiving takes roughly 5% *total*
and has been criticised for years. You would take double, with the larger half going to the
individual fundraiser.

**It is also strategically wrong for this product.** You have just built a brand around
honour, service, and doing things nobody checks. Skimming charity money is the precise
opposite, and it is the one thing that would make the honour frame read as cynical.

**Build this instead — it is a better idea than the original:**

> **The Charity Trial. 100% to the charity. You take nothing. The member takes nothing.**
> Members raise through an existing regulated platform (Enthuse, JustGiving) — you never touch
> the money, so you are not a commercial participator, not a professional fundraiser, and owe
> no statutory duties. You link the page to the trial and record it on their register entry
> permanently.

You get all the community and brand value, none of the exposure, and **a far stronger story**.
*"We take nothing"* is worth more in this brand than 4.3% of a small number — which, at your
realistic year-one scale, is not a revenue line, it is a liability with a rounding error
attached.

**Honour-consistent add-on:** make it a **conferred right** rather than a feature. Members at
a certain standing may open a Charity Trial. Rank confers duty — the highest-status mechanism
in every honour tradition.

**If you genuinely want the revenue share, that is a separate business** with separate legal
advice from a charity-law solicitor. Do not bolt it onto this one.

### ③ Three trial swaps per level → **REDESIGN. Right problem, wrong mechanism.**

**The problem is real.** A strictly sequential product with no escape hatch means one
impossible trial equals a dead account, still being billed, churning at close to 100%. The
customer research flagged this as a top objection.

**But a swap attacks your only moat.** "Fifty-seven, in order, no skipping" is the one thing
you have that free 75 Hard does not. The moment a trial can be discarded for being hard, the
order is advisory. And there is a nastier second-order effect: **swaps interact badly with
your other ideas.** With a fastest-finisher board *plus* swaps, the optimal play is to discard
every hard trial and rush the easy ones. You would have designed a game whose winning strategy
is avoiding the product.

**Three reasons the problem is smaller than it looks:**

1. **Every trial already has a SCALE clause** — a concrete smaller version that fully clears it, with no record of which version was done. That is already the accessibility answer, and it is better than a swap because the member still does the thing.
2. **The cooldowns changed the situation.** At a one-week hold between 48 and 57, "stuck" is rarely "impossible" — it is "not this week".
3. What people usually want when they say "too difficult" is *permission*, not escape. SCALE gives permission.

**Do this instead — the Duke of Edinburgh model.** DofE is your closest structural reference
(five-to-nine-month arc, 572,802 UK participants last year, nobody selling it to adults). DofE
does not let you skip a section. It lets you **choose your activity within a section.**

> Designate a small number of positions as **elective** — one per chapter, six in total —
> where the member chooses between two or three published alternatives of **equal assessed
> difficulty**, which idea ① is what makes possible.

That is choice, not escape. It is decided *before* the attempt rather than after failing, so
it never reads as quitting. Everyone still does 57, the order still holds, and the electives
become community territory — *"which did you take at 33?"* is a conversation.

**If you keep swaps anyway:** cap at three across the whole 57 rather than per level;
**record them on the register entry** (an honour system that hides its exceptions is not one);
and only ever swap into equal assessed difficulty.

### ④ Built-in forum → **DEFER. Do not build this.**

The instinct is right. Building your own forum is a well-known solo-founder trap, and there is
now a specific UK reason it is worse than it used to be.

**The Online Safety Act 2023 applies to you.** Any UK service allowing user-to-user content is
regulated. Even small, uncategorised services owe an **illegal content risk assessment**,
**content reporting**, **complaints procedures**, terms covering it, and **record-keeping**,
with Ofcom able to request information. The burden scales with size and risk, but the floor is
not zero, and it attaches the moment members can post to each other.

That duty attaches to **CTR as well** — which argues for having exactly one user-to-user
surface, not two.

**And empirically, an empty forum is worse than no forum** — it is public evidence that nobody
is here. The growth research recommended no community space below ~50 active members. You have
one member.

**Instead, in order:**
1. **Now:** a weekly **Race Bulletin** email — who cleared what, the register, one line of lore, the next drop. Twenty minutes to write, does the community job at a tenth of the cost, and owes no OSA duty because it is one-to-many.
2. **At ~50 active:** structured **per-trial discussion**, visible only to members who have reached that trial. Preserves no-spoilers, scopes moderation, and is more useful than a general forum — the conversation people want is about the trial they are on.
3. **Only if that outgrows itself:** Discord as a community space, with the app owning payment, gating and identity. Never content.

### ⑤ Hall of Fame, top ten fastest → **REJECT the speed basis. Keep the recognition.**

This is the one idea in the seven I think is actively dangerous, for four independent reasons.

1. **Safety.** Unsupervised physical activity by unscreened strangers. A visible reward for finishing fastest is a direct incentive to push through warning signs, in bad weather, ill or short of sleep. Every safety mechanism in the product spec exists to prevent exactly that, and a leaderboard undoes it in one line of UI.
2. **Liability.** You cannot disclaim liability for negligence. Publishing a board that rewards haste, on a product you accept is physical, is the kind of decision that gets read out in a claim.
3. **Integrity.** Nothing is verified. A speed board on self-reported data is a lying contest, and the fastest times get posted by whoever is least honest.
4. **It would not measure anything.** Cooldowns cap the honest maximum — 147 days minimum, a full week between the last ten. "Fastest" therefore measures **who happened to be awake when their cooldown expired.** It is a punctuality contest.

There is also a plain data-protection problem: **published names and photographs** is personal
data in a context revealing health-adjacent information, needing explicit consent, a lawful
basis and a deletion path.

**The want underneath is legitimate.** Honour traditions are excellent at recognition and none
of them use a stopwatch:

- **The Register**, in entry-number order, unranked. parkrun publishes every result and milestone and is emphatic that it is *not a race* — and it is the most successful mass-participation community in the UK.
- **The first fifty-seven finishers**, permanently recorded. Scarce, unrepeatable, ungameable by rushing because it closes once.
- **Witness lineage** — *"Bib 0142, witnessed by Bib 0061."* The strongest one: it gives finishers a role *after* finishing, which is your retention problem, and builds a lineage graph no competitor has.
- **Per-trial accounts from members who cleared it** — an ascent log, exactly as climbing does. Recognition through contribution.

### ⑥ The Community Trial Reckoning → **ADOPT THE RECIPROCITY. RESHAPE THE ARTIFACT.**

The most interesting idea of the seven, and the one most worth getting right.

**The core insight is proven.** Gating your own progress on reviewing someone else's is
well-tested:

- **Coursera peer assessment** — the closest analogue. Students grade several peers' work; aggregate peer grades correlate with instructor grades at **r ≈ 0.62**, described in the research as "fairly consistent and highly similar" to expert grading. The crowd *can* judge adequately.
- **Stack Overflow review queues** — moderation privileges earned by participation, which built the highest-quality UGC corpus on the internet with a tiny paid staff.
- **Slashdot meta-moderation** — moderating the moderators, structurally almost identical to your proposal.

**Reciprocity also solves your hardest problem, which is not verification — it is that nobody
talks in a new community.** Making contribution a condition of progress guarantees a floor of
activity. That is the real prize, and why I would keep the mechanic even while changing what
gets submitted.

**The problems, all solvable:**

**(a) Deadlock.** If advancing requires voting and there is nothing to vote on, everyone stops.
With 20 members on staggered cooldowns that is not hypothetical, it is month one's default
state. **Fix:** the obligation is *"witness when there is something to witness"* — an empty
queue never blocks anyone, and the debt is paid at the next opportunity. Never let an empty
queue hold a paying member still.

**(b) Drive-by participation.** Compulsory review produces minimum-effort review — the
best-documented failure mode of MOOC peer grading. **Fix:** ask for one sentence, not a score.
A written attestation cannot be produced by clicking, and it is worth reading.

**(c) Video is the expensive choice and I would not start there.** Storage and egress your
current free-tier infrastructure cannot absorb, visual-content moderation, much heavier OSA
and GDPR exposure (faces, homes, children in shot, location inference) — and it changes
behaviour, because people perform for cameras. **Start with a written account and optionally
one photograph.**

**(d) Rings.** Reciprocal upvoting. Mostly dissolved by the witness reframing (no score to
inflate), and visible lineage helps — a closed ring is obvious in a public graph.

**(e) It changes your legal position.** Member-submitted content about physical activity is
personal data, arguably health data, plus full OSA duties. The single biggest compliance
step-up in the plan, and another reason to launch without it and add it deliberately.

**What I would build:**

> **The Reckoning.** When you clear a trial you write a short account — what you did, where,
> what it cost you. It goes on the register under your bib.
>
> Before your next trial opens, you **stand witness** to another member's account: you read it
> and you sign it. Signing says *"I have read this and I take it as given."* It is not a score
> and there is no rejection.
>
> There is one **flag**, used rarely, meaning *"this does not read as honest."* Flags go to
> Race Control, never to the member, and stay private. Two flags on one account is a
> conversation, not an automatic anything.
>
> Your account carries the bib numbers of everyone who witnessed it, permanently.

Everything you wanted — community as the spine, forced reciprocity, mutual accountability,
progression gated on participation — with none of the judging, most of the cost removed, and
the honour frame strengthened rather than contradicted.

---

### ⑦ Big, tense trial reveals → **ADOPT. Cheap and correct.**

Pure upside, costs almost nothing, and it is what the cooldown makes possible: a week's wait
is only frustrating if the thing at the end is flat.

Keep it in register voice, not game-show voice. Tension from withholding and formality, not
animation. A sealed briefing that opens once; a countdown that reads as an interval rather
than a timer; the number before the title.

**The strongest version costs nothing:** you already withhold trial content server-side. Show
the trial's **number and grade** while it is still locked, and nothing else. Anticipation
needs an object.

## 13.2 How the seven interact

**The bad interaction.** Swaps + speed ranking + vote-gating combine into a game whose optimal
strategy is: discard hard trials, rush easy ones, farm reciprocal votes. Each is defensible
alone; together they are exploitable. Dropping the speed board and reshaping swaps into
electives removes it.

**The good interaction.** Difficulty grading (①) makes electives (③) possible, because equal-
difficulty alternatives require knowing the difficulties. Grading feeds the reveal (⑦),
because a published grade is the object anticipation attaches to. The Reckoning (⑥) generates
the per-trial accounts that make grades trustworthy. **Four of the seven reinforce each other.
Build them in that order.**

## 13.3 The plan

### Phase 0 — now, pre-launch: **The Grading**
*The only phase that cannot be done later.*

- Recruit **20–40 pilot members**, free, with an explicit job: clear the trials early and grade them.
- Confer **bibs 001–057** on this cohort. Never issued again.
- Two-axis grading — physical demand and life friction — plus completion rate and time-to-clear.
- **Reorder the 57 on the results.** That is the deliverable.
- Publish the final grades permanently.
- No UGC, no forum, no charity, no video. Nothing here triggers a legal duty.

**This also attacks your cold-start problem**, which the market research called the primary
risk ahead of churn and pricing. Forty people who graded the product are a community. Forty
people who bought it are a list.

### Phase 1 — launch: **The Register and the Reveal**
- The Register in entry-number order. No ranking, no times, ever.
- Big reveals with published grades.
- Weekly Race Bulletin email.
- First-fifty-seven finishers recorded permanently.
- Still no UGC — launch with zero moderation burden.

### Phase 2 — at ~50 active members: **The Reckoning**
- Written accounts, one optional photo. No video yet.
- Witness-to-advance, with the empty-queue rule so it can never deadlock.
- Private flags to Race Control.
- Witness lineage on the register.
- **Before this ships:** OSA risk assessment, reporting and complaints mechanisms, terms covering UGC, retention and deletion policy. Do this deliberately, not accidentally.
- Per-trial discussion scoped to members who have reached that trial.

### Phase 3 — once it is genuinely working: **Service**
- Charity Trials, 100% to charity, through a regulated platform you never touch money in. Conferred as a right earned by standing.
- Electives at one position per chapter.
- Video in the Reckoning, if members ask and the cost is modelled.
- Discord, if per-trial discussion outgrows itself.

## 13.4 The three I would push back on hardest

1. **Do not take a percentage of charity money.** Criminal-liability regime, an agreement needed with every charity, and brand-fatal in a way 4.3% of a small number cannot justify. Take nothing and say so loudly.
2. **Do not rank by speed.** Safety, liability, integrity — and it measures nothing once cooldowns exist. The recognition want is real and there are four better ways to serve it.
3. **Do not launch with video UGC.** Largest cost, largest compliance jump, largest moderation burden — and text attestations serve the community purpose better, because they can actually be read.

## 13.5 The one I would push hardest *for*

**Run the pre-launch grading cohort.** It is the only idea that is strictly time-limited, it
converts your worst problem (nobody knows this exists) into your best asset (people who built
it), it costs nothing, it carries no legal exposure, and it produces the data three of the
other six ideas depend on.

If you do one thing from this document, do that.

## 13.6 The verification problem

Rob: *"the hardest part... actually confirming the trial was complete unless we make the
trials uniform in the fact that they can be easily cross checked like winning a game of chess
online or completing a sudoku problem."*

That instinct is right about the mechanism and wrong about the target. It is worth separating
those, because the mechanism is usable and the target is not.

### Why "make it checkable like chess" cannot work here

Chess and sudoku are verifiable because **the activity happens inside the system**. The move
is the record. There is no gap between doing and reporting.

Physical activity in the world has an irreducible gap: something happens, then someone tells
the system about it. Every verification method is an attempt to narrow that gap, and none
closes it. The companies that tried hardest are the evidence:

| Who | Method | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| **Strava** | GPS and sensor data | Segment leaderboards are routinely gamed by driving routes and spoofing GPS. Strava largely stopped fighting it and made the product social instead. |
| **Zwift** | Power meters indoors | For competitive racing they had to add **dual recording**, filmed weigh-ins and height verification — and still had cheating scandals. An enormous apparatus for a tiny elite. |
| **The Conqueror** | Step and distance tracking | Reports a 91.4% completion rate, which tells you they are not really policing it. |
| **75 Hard** | Nothing at all | A million claimed completions. |
| **parkrun** | Barcode scanned at a physical finish line | **Actually works** — but requires you to be at a place, at a time, with staff. |
| **Duke of Edinburgh** | A named **Assessor** signs off each section | Works, scales to 572,802 UK participants, and is a human, not a sensor. |

**The pattern: sensor verification fails against motivated cheats, and presence or human
attestation works.** You cannot build parkrun's finish line. You can build DofE's assessor.

### The reframe that makes this tractable

> **Stop trying to verify that the trial was done. Verify the shape of the engagement instead.**

That *is* machine-checkable, and it is what your product already half does. Consider a HOLD
trial — seven consecutive days of one action, logged each morning:

- You cannot fake seven consecutive daily check-ins retroactively. The timestamps either exist across seven distinct days or they do not.
- The system cannot know whether the member drank the glass of water. It knows, to the second, that they showed up seven mornings running.
- **That is a real, uncheatable fact, and it is arguably the more meaningful one.**

This is exactly Duolingo's mechanic. Duolingo does not verify that you learned Spanish. It
verifies that you turned up, and the product works because turning up is the thing that
matters. Your cooldowns already put you in this territory: a 195-day minimum is a fact about
elapsed time that no member can compress.

### The four tiers, and which trials get which

Your trials are not uniform — and that is an asset here, not a problem. Different types admit
different verification, so assign deliberately and **publish the tier on each trial**:

| Tier | Method | Fits | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Logged** | Timestamped check-ins across required days | HOLD trials | Machine-verifiable and genuinely hard to fake |
| **Witnessed** | A named person confirms, DofE assessor style | SIGNAL trials, and every RED trial 48–57 | Strong, social, scales, costs nothing |
| **Produced** | An artifact exists — a written account, a finished thing, a photograph | MARK, CRAFT | Weak as proof, strong as commitment |
| **Attested** | The member's word, nothing more | Everything else | Zero cost, and the brand's whole premise |

Publishing the tier is the honest move and it makes the register mean more, not less: *"this
one is witnessed"* is information, and a trial that says *"attested"* is telling the truth
about itself.

**Note what falls out of this.** The RED chapter already requires a named check-in person
before the member starts — that person exists for safety reasons. **They are already the
assessor.** Asking them to confirm afterwards costs nothing and adds a verification tier to
exactly the trials where it matters most.

### The anti-cheat insight

The instinct is to build detection. The better move is to remove the reward.

> **Ask who a cheat is hurting.** In a product with no prize, cheating hurts only the cheat.
> That is why 75 Hard does not care, and why it does not need to.

Cheating becomes rational the moment there is something to win. The two things that would
have created that incentive were the speed leaderboard and fast merch — and both are now
gone: the board is dropped, and cooldowns mean the first milestone is 25 days away minimum
for a low-value patch.

**So the fair-play strategy is: keep the prize small and the record honest, and do not build a
detection apparatus you would then have to staff.** Zwift's experience is instructive — the
verification burden scales with the value of winning, and yours should stay near zero because
there is nothing to win but the record itself.

The one backstop worth having is the private flag in the Reckoning. Not a rejection
mechanism, not a court — a quiet signal to Race Control that something does not read as
honest.

## 13.7 Making the trials interactive — five mechanisms

Ordered by value against cost.

### 1. Beta — advice from those who have cleared it
Climbing's word for how to do a route, passed between climbers. Members who have cleared a
trial can leave a short note for those who have not yet reached it.

**Why this is the best one:** it costs nothing, it makes every trial page a living document
that improves over time, it gives cleared members a reason to return to a trial they have
finished, and it is *the* proven mechanic in the one community whose grading system you are
already borrowing. It also softens difficulty without changing the trial, which is the thing
swaps were trying to do.

Gate it the obvious way: you can only read beta for a trial you have reached, and only write
it for one you have cleared.

### 2. Synchronised cohorts
Everyone who starts in a given month moves through roughly together.

**This solves three problems at once:** the empty-community problem (there is always someone
at your trial), the Reckoning deadlock (there is always something in the queue to witness),
and the reveal (a whole cohort unlocks a trial in the same week, so it is an event rather than
a solitary moment).

parkrun's entire social mechanism is that everyone runs at 9am on Saturday. Simultaneity is
what turns individual activity into a community.

### 3. Grading never closes
The pre-launch cohort sets the grade; every member who clears a trial thereafter adjusts it.
Grades stay live and shift as the population changes — exactly how climbing consensus works.

Every member gets a voice on every trial, forever, for one tap. And it gives you a genuine
early-warning signal: a trial whose grade climbs sharply is one to review.

### 4. Pairs
Two members pair up and must clear the same trial within the same window or the pair breaks.
Proven accountability mechanic, inherently viral (you cannot pair without recruiting), and it
fits the brand — a race has pacers.

### 5. Witness lineage
*"Bib 0142, witnessed by Bib 0061."* Standing as a witness is a right earned by completing,
which gives finishers a role after they finish — your retention problem — and builds a visible
lineage graph that no competitor has and that becomes more valuable as the register lengthens.
