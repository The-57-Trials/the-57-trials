## 1.1 The mission, in one line

**Give people one hard, finite thing to finish — and permanent proof they finished it.**

The reason this exists beyond revenue: almost nothing in self-improvement ever *ends*.
Gym memberships don't end. Streak apps don't end — they just break and shame you. Habit
trackers run forever, so there is never a moment where you are done.

The 57 Trials ends. There are exactly 57. When you clear the fifty-seventh, you have
finished a thing, and your bib number says so permanently. That finiteness is the actual
product. The challenges are the mechanism.

**What we are selling:** not fitness, not discipline, not transformation. A finishable
ordeal with a witness.

## 1.2 What "won" looks like

Deliberately unglamorous numbers. A solo operator with a day job should be measured
against sustainability, not against venture-scale growth.

### 12 months
| | Target | Why this number |
|---|---|---|
| Active Circuit Pass holders | **250** | ≈£1,250 MRR — covers all costs and pays the founder something real |
| Trials written | **57 of 57** | Content runway is the operational risk; being finished removes it |
| First finishers | **at least 1** | Proof the whole arc is completable, and the finisher bib exists in the world |
| Median trials cleared per paying member | **> 15** | Members reaching the first merch milestone means the format holds attention |
| Refund + chargeback rate | **< 5%** | The honest signal that expectations match reality |

"Won" at 12 months is: **the thing pays for itself, the content is finished, and people
are completing it.** Not scale.

### 3 years
- **The 57 Trials is a recognised format**, not just a website — people say "I'm on trial 34" the way they say "I'm doing 75 Hard".
- **A second circuit exists.** The 57 is finite by design, which means finishers churn *by design*. A follow-on circuit is how the model survives its own completion.
- **Merch is a deliberate line**, designed and margin-positive, rather than a bundled cost.
- **The founder is no longer the only operator** — at minimum, fulfilment and support are off his desk.

**The strategic tension to hold onto:** a finite product with an end has built-in churn.
Every finisher is a cancelled subscription. That is not a bug — it is the reason the
product is honest — but the three-year plan has to answer *what comes after 57* or the
business caps itself.

## 1.3 Values and brand ethos

Five, and they should be visible in the product rather than written on an About page.

**1. No skipping ahead.**
The sequence is sacred and enforced in the database, not merely requested. This is the
brand's spine — it is why the Discord version failed and why this exists.

**2. Earned, not bought.**
Merch arrives because you reached a checkpoint, never because you paid for it. Your bib
number is issued once and never reissued. Your rank is your own.

**3. No hype.**
No transformation promises, no before-and-afters, no "unlock your potential". The tone is
an event marshal, not a motivational speaker. This is also a legal position — outcome
claims create both advertising and consumer-protection exposure.

**4. Finish what you start.**
The product's whole thesis. Which means the copy must never reward speed or punish a slow
finish. **This conflicts with the current leaderboard**, which ranks by who got there
first — see §10.

**5. The number is yours.**
Bib numbers start at 58; 001–057 are reserved and unexplained. Serial `5757` appears in
the footer and is never explained anywhere. Lore is stronger unexplained.

### On "The Primes" — get this right before printing anything

**5 and 7 are prime. 57 is not** — it factors to 3 × 19.

So the brand must say *"five and seven, the primes"* and must never say *"57, a prime
number"*. The first is true and quietly elegant: two primes standing side by side, which
is exactly what the number is. The second is false, trivially checkable, and would be
picked apart immediately by precisely the pedantic, detail-loving audience this brand
attracts.

There is a redemption available if you want it. 57 is famously nicknamed the
**"Grothendieck prime"**, after the mathematician who reportedly offered it when asked for
an example of a prime. Leaned on knowingly — as a joke the informed audience is in on —
that is genuinely good lore. Leaned on ignorantly, it is an error. The difference is
whether the brand shows it knows.

## 1.4 The number that tells you it's working

One primary metric. Everything else is diagnostic.

> ### Circuit Pass holders retained past month 3
> Not signups. Not MRR. Not total members.

**Why this one.** Signups measure marketing. MRR measures a moment. Month-3 retention
measures whether the *product* works — and it sits just past the point where the first
merch milestone (trial 15) has been earned, so it captures both "did they stay" and "did
the reward mean anything".

It is also the number the whole financial model hinges on. If members churn before month
3, a fast completer can consume a £-value merch item having paid one or two subscription
months, and the unit economics invert.

### The supporting five
| Metric | Question it answers | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Trial-06 conversion | Does the second paywall kill momentum? | The Pulse — "stalled at 06" |
| Visitor → £10 conversion | Does the landing page work? | Needs analytics (not yet installed) |
| Median trials cleared | Does the format hold attention? | The Pulse — "avg cleared" |
| Merch cost per member | Are fast completers unprofitable? | Manual, against §5 model |
| Refund + chargeback rate | Do expectations match reality? | Stripe dashboard |

**The one to watch first, at launch:** *stalled at 06*. Every member sitting there has
paid £10, hit the wall, and declined to subscribe. That count is the single most
informative number in the first month, and it is on the dashboard for exactly that reason.
